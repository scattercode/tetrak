# Fixture sources

Two things live in this directory, and they have different goals.

**The published subset** — the nine fixtures the research pages and the
walkthrough show — holds to a theme: **the Los Angeles stage and its picture
palaces**, a vaudeville poster, an LA theatrical trade weekly, two linen
postcards of Hollywood movie palaces, a roadshow souvenir programme, a theatre
playbill and a newspaper display ad. The theme is a presentation choice. It
makes the walkthrough read as one collection rather than a grab bag, and the
period follows from needing material that is safely out of copyright to publish
— not from any interest in a date range for its own sake.

**The wider corpus** has the opposite goal: variety, so that tuning does not
overfit to a handful of documents. The product has to serve archives full of
unruly ephemera *and* collections of plain black text on white, and a corpus
that is all ephemera cannot tell us whether we manage the second. Nothing here
is bounded by date, place or subject. Items are worth adding when they break
something new.

`splits.toml` is where the two are kept apart: publication is opt-in, so the
corpus can grow without the research pages growing with it.

Every item is in the public domain in the United States. The rights reasoning
for each is given below. None carries an attribution requirement, but crediting
the holding institution is good practice and costs nothing.

## Modifications

- `kar-mi-troupe-poster.jpg` was downscaled from the source 4104 × 5116 to
  1123 × 1400 so that it sits in the same resolution band as the rest of the
  set. Resolution materially changes OCR scores, so fixtures are kept
  comparable. No cropping was applied — the Library of Congress colour
  calibration bar and ruler remain in frame, which is representative of real
  archive scans.
- `camera-1919.pdf` was cropped from the Internet Archive's
  912-page volume scan with `qpdf --pages . 7-16`, taking it from 165 MB to
  3.5 MB. Pages 1–6 of that scan are the binding and blank leaves — they carry
  no text at all, confirmed page by page — and pages 7–16 are pages 1–10 of the
  6 April 1919 issue, so the extract is a whole run of a single issue rather
  than an arbitrary slice. Page extraction only: qpdf copies the page objects
  untouched, so the rasters are the Internet Archive's own, unresampled.

  No downscaling, unlike the poster above. The pipeline renders PDFs rather
  than reading their embedded rasters, and at the default 200 dpi this comes
  out at 1966 × 2724 against `king-of-kings-souvenir-1927.pdf`'s 1542 × 2134 —
  the same band, reached the same way. The ~1400 px normalisation applies to
  the still images, where the source resolution is what OCR sees.

- `film-mercury-1928.pdf` and
  `film-spectator-1927.pdf` were cropped the same way, from the
  Internet Archive's 220-page and 466-page volume scans respectively. Neither
  has the blank leaves *Camera!* had — both open straight onto an issue front
  page — so the extract is pages 1-10 in each case, again a whole run of one
  issue. 20 MB and 38 MB down to 1.2 MB and 0.8 MB.

- The four Library of Congress posters were downscaled from source to a 1400 px
  long edge, the same treatment and the same reason as the Victorina poster
  above: 4126 × 5110 → 1130 × 1400 (Bancroft), 1800 × 2687 → 938 × 1400
  (Thurston), 2629 × 2032 → 1400 × 1082 (*A parlor match*, the one landscape
  item) and 2172 × 3011 → 1010 × 1400 (*Over the fence*). Resized once from the
  full original rather than from a Wikimedia thumbnail: the thumbnailer snaps
  to bucket widths — 1280, 1920 — so asking it for 1130 returns 1280, and
  resizing that again would resample twice for no reason.
- The four Tichnor postcards were fetched at 1400 px wide, matching the two
  already here. Fronts only; each item also has a reverse, which is a different
  OCR problem and is not included.

- The three plain-text items were cropped from larger scans the same way as the
  trade papers: `christmas-carol-1887.pdf` is pages 15–24 of a 168-page book,
  chosen to skip the frontispiece, contents and plate list and land on running
  prose; `naca-technical-note-1952.pdf` is pages 1–10 of 14; and
  `st-cloud-tribune-1924.pdf` is the whole issue, which is only eight pages.

- `carthay-circle-postcard-back.png` was converted from the source JPEG so the
  set exercises the PNG path. No resampling.
- The postcards were fetched at 1400 px wide via the Digital Commonwealth IIIF
  endpoint. Everything else is unmodified from the source.
- The two TIFFs are unmodified. They arrive as single-frame, 8-bit RGB, LZW
  compressed, at 573 × 1200 (playbill) and 1477 × 1536 (Kinema ad) — close
  enough to the ~1400 px band the rest of the set was normalised to that
  resampling would cost more in artefacts than it would buy in comparability.

## Ground truth

`expected/<stem>.md` is the reference transcript every backend is scored
against. Where it came from matters as much as the rights position does, and
for the same reason: a number is only as good as what produced it.

**Most of these were written by Claude, and Claude is scored against them.**
`tools/generate_expected.py` sends each image to the Anthropic vision API and
commits the result. That makes the `claude` column of the benchmark a measure
of self-consistency rather than accuracy, and it is why `CLAUDE.md` says not to
quote Claude's average as a measurement of Claude. It also means every *other*
engine is scored against a reference that may itself be wrong.

**The correction on 28 September 2026 shows the size of the effect.** The
generated transcript for `bancroft-magician-poster` had missed the printer's
imprint — "Springer & Welty Co. / LITH.N.Y. / BROADWAY & 31 ST.", in ornate
script and partly illegible at the corpus resolution — along with the
sub-title. Checked against a higher-resolution copy of the same mark and
corrected by hand, the fixture's best character similarity rose from 0.3277 to
0.3763 and its word recall fell from 0.200 to 0.125. Both moves are right:
Tesseract had been reading text the reference did not ask for, so it gained
credit it was owed, and the longer reference then exposed how much of the
script it cannot read. The old reference was flattering word recall by leaving
text off the page.

So the status below is not bookkeeping. An unverified transcript is a known
unknown in every score derived from it, including the band fit in
`evaluation/ocr/calibration/`.

### What goes in a reference transcript

One rule, arrived at while checking the 28 September batch and worth stating
because every fixture raises it:

> **Record text whose marks are present in this scan. Outside evidence may be
> used to *resolve* those marks. It may never supply text whose marks are
> absent.**

The test is resolving versus inventing, not knowing versus not knowing. A
cataloguer who has found a better copy of the same sign is allowed to use it
to settle a reading; they are not allowed to transcribe from it.

Worked cases, all from 28 September 2026:

- `graumans-chinese-theatre` — the marquee reads `SYLVIA SI` and then the
  frame cuts it. The film is *You Only Live Once* and its star was Sylvia
  Sidney, so the word is certainly `SIDNEY`, and the letters are certainly not
  in the image. The transcript stops at `SI`.
- `hollywood-boulevard-east` — the painted wall sign is `Furs / Bess Schlank /
  Gowns & Wraps`. Only the script name is legible unaided; a photograph of the
  same building resolves the two smaller lines, and the marks for them are
  there in the artwork. They go in.
- `hollywood-boulevard-east`, again — the rooftop board reads `BENEDICT /
  PORTRAITS / STUDIO AND HOME / PHOTOGRAPHY` in that same photograph, and the
  line falls *inside* it. The top two lines are painted as lettering and are
  transcribed. The last two are bands of near-uniform horizontal streaking
  with no vertical stroke structure: this is a linen card, an artist's
  rendering, and below a certain size the painter suggested text rather than
  setting it. There are no letters there to read, so they stay out.

  That the line runs through the middle of one sign is the point. It is not a
  judgement about which sign matters, or about what the building said — the
  photograph settles that and is a different document. It is a judgement,
  made with the scan at magnification, about where the artist stopped drawing
  letters. Anyone revisiting it should look at the image rather than reason
  from the photograph.

**A borderline case, recorded rather than smoothed over.** On
`hollywood-boulevard-east` the rooftop board's second line was included as
`PORTRAITS`, and the cataloguer's own account of it was that `PORTR` reads
cleanly and the remainder is a blur that a reader knowing the answer can
resolve and one who does not, probably cannot.

Three engines were run on the scan afterwards to see whether any recovers it.
None does: `tesseract` gets fragments, `claude` reads `BENEDICT` and
`Bess Schlank` but neither `PORTRAITS` nor `Furs` — on two independent passes,
the original generation and a later check — and `paddle-vl` reads only the
caption line and the serial numbers.

That was not treated as a reason to drop it, and the reasoning is the point.
**What an engine reads is evidence about the engine, not about the document.**
A reference set to what today's engines recover can never show an engine
improving, because the ceiling moves with the floor; and an engine that later
reads those letters correctly would be *penalised* for the extra words. The
letters are on the card, so they are in the transcript, and the two-word gap
between the card and the best available engine is now a measurement rather
than an omission.

**The document's own errors are preserved, not corrected.** On
`hollywood-music-box-playbill-1926`, line 42 reads `On Sale at Music Box
Theatr e,GRanite 4152` — the space misplaced from after the comma to inside
the word. The same playbill sets `Imperial Theatre,` correctly twice, so this
is the compositor's slip on one line, and it is transcribed as it stands.

Anyone checking this file will want to fix it. Do not. The reference records
what the document shows, and a typesetting error is something the document
shows; an engine that reads `Theatr e,GRanite` has read the playbill
correctly, and one that "helpfully" emits `Theatre, GRanite` has not. Silently
normalising it would credit the second and penalise the first. This is the
same rule as the two cases above, applied to a document that is wrong rather
than to one that is illegible.

(`GRanite 4152` is correct as it stands, incidentally — a 1920s telephone
exchange name, where the capitalised letters are the ones dialled.)

**Line breaks and end-of-line hyphens are preserved.** The single most
valuable correction of the 28 September review was on
`inside-facts-1930-page-six`, and it changed no words at all. Claude had
reflowed the newspaper's columns into continuous paragraphs and joined every
hyphenated line-break: `No-` / `vember` became `November`, and 26 others like
it. Restoring the page's own line breaking moved the fixture's best character
similarity from 0.4739 to **0.7270**, and its plain-Tesseract score from
0.4536 to **0.6838**.

The reason the effect is so large is that the reference was wrong in a way
that scaled with the document. Line position itself does not matter --
`normalise()` collapses all whitespace, so a newline and a space are the same
to both metrics -- but the *hyphen* is a character. Against a de-hyphenated
reference, an engine that correctly read `sen-` and `tient` on two lines
scored two wrong tokens for word recall and a character mismatch for
similarity, 27 times over. Tesseract's raw output on this page contains
exactly those 27 hyphenations, so it was being penalised for reading the page
correctly.

Note the asymmetry with the shipped pipeline. `tetrak_ocr.layout` provides a
`dehyphenate()` helper, on the entirely sound view that a *reader* wants
`November`. That is right for output and wrong for scoring, and the two are
currently reconciled only by accident: `dehyphenate()` is called by two
analysis tools and is not wired into the harness. If it is ever moved into
the scoring path, every reference in this directory becomes wrong at once.

Why it matters in both directions. Text wrongly omitted costs an engine credit
it earned — that was `bancroft-magician-poster`, where the missing imprint was
worth +0.05 character similarity once restored. Text wrongly added penalises
every engine for failing to read something that was never legible, and quietly
lowers the ceiling the calibration is fitted against. The second is the easier
mistake to make when a good photograph is open beside the scan.

### A caution about `kar-mi-troupe-poster` and character similarity

Measured 28 September 2026, while checking whether a higher-resolution scan
would be worth adding. It would not, and the reason is worth recording.

Four `claude` runs, the same image, the same reference transcript:

| run | character similarity | word recall | words returned |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 0.9884 | 0.9903 | — |
| 2 | 0.7658 | 0.9855 | 206 |
| 3 | 0.4804 | 0.9807 | 208 |
| 4 | 0.9828 | 0.9855 | 208 |

The engine reads the same text, in the same quantity, every time. Word recall
holds within 0.010. Character similarity moves by **0.50**, because the only
thing changing is the order in which fifteen scattered vignettes get
described — and this poster has no canonical reading order for an engine to
get right. `benchmark.csv` records 0.2514 for this cell, which is below the
whole observed range: that run caught an unusually unlucky ordering and the
figure has been published ever since.

Two consequences:

- **Do not read the per-fixture `claude` character similarity on this fixture
  as a measurement.** It is a sample from a wide distribution. Word recall is
  the stable statistic here.
- **The band fit is unaffected.** It uses `tesseract`, whose output order is
  deterministic for a given PSM, and it fits on regret within a fixture rather
  than across fixtures. The instability is a property of generative engines on
  orderless material, not of the calibration.

Resolution was tested at the same time and is not the problem. Tesseract
cannot read this poster at 4104 x 5116 either — eighteen configurations at
native resolution return about 170 lines of noise off the chromolithograph and
six broken fragments — and Claude already reads it at 0.99 from the downscaled
copy. What defeats Tesseract is the display type on curved and arched
baselines over dense illustration, not the pixel count.

### Keeping this current

Mark a fixture human-verified when someone has read the image and the
transcript side by side — not when it merely looks plausible. Say what changed,
and what the correction was checked against, because "verified" without a
source is a claim rather than evidence.

A corrected transcript invalidates that fixture's rows in
`calibration/sweep.csv` and every score derived from them. Re-measure just that
fixture rather than the whole corpus:

```bash
python -m evaluation.ocr.calibration.sweep --fixture <stem> --save
python -m evaluation.ocr.calibration.fit --split wide-corpus
```

The benchmark moves too, and `evaluation/ocr/harness.py --backend <name> --save
--merge` re-measures one backend without re-running the rest.

## Notes

- **The Chinese Theatre postcard can be dated from its own marquee, to 1937 or
  later.** Digital Commonwealth gives the Tichnor linen cards a blanket
  c. 1930–45, which is a printing range for the series rather than a date for
  this photograph. The marquee reads `SYLVIA SIL…` / `IN 'YOU O…` / `ALSO
  DAN…`, truncated where the frame cuts it. The feature is *You Only Live
  Once*, Fritz Lang's film for Walter Wanger, which opened at Grauman's
  Chinese Theatre on 27 January 1937 — so the photograph cannot predate that,
  and the card's range narrows to c. 1937–45.

  Two things about it are unresolved, and are recorded here rather than
  guessed at in the transcript:

  **The star's name, and why the transcript stops at `SYLVIA SI`.** *You Only
  Live Once* starred Sylvia **Sidney**, with Henry Fonda, so the marquee
  almost certainly reads `SYLVIA SIDNEY`. The next letter on the scan is
  cropped far enough that `D` and `L` cannot be told apart, and it is left out
  rather than inferred. That is the rule this corpus follows: the reference
  records what the image shows, not what the cataloguer knows. Writing
  `SIDNEY` from external knowledge would credit an engine for reading letters
  that are not there to read, which is the same error as the omissions
  corrected on 28 September, pointing the other way.

  **The second feature.** `ALSO DAN…` is cut off and the title is not
  recoverable from this scan. Candidates in release around the same weeks,
  none confirmed and offered only as leads for anyone with access to the
  Los Angeles trade listings: *Dangerous Number* (MGM), *Dangerously Yours*
  (Fox), *Dangerous Holiday* (Republic), *Dance, Charlie, Dance* (Warner
  Bros.), all 1937. Resolving it would date the card to a particular week
  rather than a year, and would need the LA Public Library's newspaper
  holdings — it is not answerable from the image.

- **1930 is a recent public-domain entry.** *Inside Facts* fell into the public
  domain on 1 January 2026 under the US 95-year term. That is the least-aged
  item in the set by a wide margin.
- **The playbill's rights note is weaker than its date.** The Homestead Museum
  states only that "copyright has not been assigned to the museum", which is
  not a public-domain declaration. It does not need to be: a US work published
  in 1926 is out of copyright on the date alone, more than 95 years having
  passed. Crediting "Workman and Temple Family Homestead Museum Collection, USC
  Digital Library" is good practice rather than a requirement.
- **The Kinema ad's scan has no confirmed producer.** The file came from a
  genealogy/newspaper-archive-style export, and which site produced this
  particular scan cannot be established from the file itself. The underlying
  1920 content is unambiguously public domain either way: US law recognises no
  separate copyright in a straightforward photographic reproduction of a flat
  public-domain work (*Bridgeman Art Library v. Corel*, 36 F. Supp. 2d 191).
  Re-sourcing the same page from Chronicling America or a library archive would
  remove any residual doubt about the scan — see the refetching note below on
  why that has to be done by hand.
- **The three trade-paper extracts have no ground truth yet.** Added 21 August
  2026: *Camera!* (1919), *The Film Mercury* (1928) and *The Film Spectator*
  (1927). Nothing scores them until a matching file exists in `expected/` —
  `available_pairs()` only pairs fixtures that have one — and each needs
  classifying in `splits.toml` before the calibration work will run, since that
  test fails on any unclassified fixture, which is what it is for.

  What they add is breadth in one failure mode rather than a new one. The
  corpus already has dense multi-column newsprint in *Inside Facts* (1930),
  where the locals score 0.03-0.40 against Claude's 0.94-1.00. These are three
  more mastheads and column grids, nine to eleven years earlier, and *The Film
  Spectator* is the odd one of the four: Welford Beaton's criticism runs as
  long prose columns rather than the classified-style blocks of the other
  three, so it tests reading order on a different shape of page.

- **The easy end of the range is now covered, and it measures as easy.** Until
  21 August 2026 every fixture was ephemera, so nothing exercised the ordinary
  case — black text on white, one column, plain type — and nothing would have
  noticed if the pipeline regressed on it. Plain `tesseract` on the two
  additions, with no tuning:

  *A Christmas Carol* returns "MARLEY'S GHOST. MAn®y was dead: to begin with.
  There is no doubt whatever about that. The register of his burial was signed
  by the elergyman, the clerk, the undertaker, and the chief mourner." — two
  errors in forty words, against fixtures elsewhere in this set where the same
  engine returns nothing at all. The NACA note is comparable once past the
  library stamp on its cover.

  That gives the calibration work something to fit at the easy end, where it
  previously had nothing, and it gives the quality gate a case that should
  always pass.

- **`st-cloud-tribune-1924.pdf` is not an easy case, despite being a newspaper.**
  Eight pages of dense small-type broadsheet, and plain `tesseract` scrambles it
  much as it does *Inside Facts*. It is here for breadth of *material* rather
  than to test the plain path — a complete ordinary local paper, which is what
  a county archive is full of. It also renders larger than everything else,
  2369 × 3285 against a set that otherwise sits between 1450 and 1970 wide,
  because a broadsheet page is physically bigger. That does not break
  comparability: PDFs are rendered at a fixed 200 dpi, so pixels per character
  — the thing OCR actually depends on — stay in line.

- **TIFF is not covered.** The pipeline advertises TIFF support and no fixture
  exercises it. Converting one item would close the gap.

## Refetching

Internet Archive, Wikimedia Commons and Digital Commonwealth are all
scriptable — stable download URLs, JSON APIs, no bot protection. Three
practical notes, each of which cost time on 21 August 2026:

- **Digital Commonwealth needs the manifest first.** The URL previously
  recorded here —
  `https://iiif.digitalcommonwealth.org/iiif/2/commonwealth:<id>/full/1400,/0/default.jpg`
  — now returns 404 for *every* id, including the ones already in this corpus,
  so it is not that the candidate ids were wrong. An item id and its image id
  are different things. Resolve
  `https://ark.digitalcommonwealth.org/ark:/50959/<item-id>/manifest`, take the
  image service `@id` from it, and request `/full/1400,/0/default.jpg` from
  that. `2n49tj420` (the Carthay premiere postcard) resolves to image
  `2n49tj438`.
- **Wikimedia requires a descriptive User-Agent.** The default `urllib` one is
  refused with HTTP 403. Any string naming the project and a contact URL works.
- **Internet Archive's canonical download URL can 500.** The 302 from
  `archive.org/download/<id>/<file>` landed on a node returning HTTP 500 for
  *The Film Mercury*. The `d1`, `d2` and `server` fields in
  `https://archive.org/metadata/<id>` name the hosts directly, and the `dir`
  field gives the path — which is not `/0/items/`.

`loc.gov` and Chronicling America sit behind Cloudflare bot protection and
return HTTP 403 to `curl` even with a browser user-agent, so anything sourced
there has to be downloaded by hand through a browser. This is why the Library
of Congress poster is taken from the Wikimedia Commons mirror rather than
directly, and why no Chronicling America newspaper page is included despite the
*Los Angeles Herald* being an obvious thematic fit.
